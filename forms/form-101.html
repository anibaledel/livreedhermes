Form 101 of 128 — hexagram 4 (King Wen no. 15) and its complement hexagram 59 (King Wen no. 10)

A form gathers four grids: hexagram 4 (King Wen no. 15), its complement hexagram 59 (King Wen no. 10), each in both couples of the family (the YANG couple, orange base, and the YIN couple, magenta base). The four draw the same shape, up to tints and complement.

Tradition

The Yi Jing judgement and image already on this site, quoted as they stand (source: scripts/extract-hexagram-data.js (tirage-livree-hermes.html)). This is not an attribution to the form.

hexagram 4 (King Wen no. 15) — Modesty

Judgement — Alchemy. Self-effacement nourishes the transmutation within the crucible. Nothing truly transforms without some part of the self consenting to dissolve in the process. This line says that humility is not a passive virtue: it is the active condition of all deep transformation, the crucible where the old dissolves so the new may take shape.

Image. Within the Earth stands the mountain: the image of modesty. What is highest here allows itself to be contained by what surrounds it, never seeking to dominate the landscape by height alone. This is the figure of greatness that effaces itself willingly. Thus the noble one reduces what is excessive and increases what is lacking, weighing all things with equity: modesty is not smallness, it is strength secure enough in itself that it need not show itself.

hexagram 59 (King Wen no. 10) — Treading

Judgement — Fullness. Every step placed upon the tiger's tail without wavering. Danger is not always to be avoided — sometimes it must be crossed, provided one walks through it with total attention, without a single misstep. This line says that caution is not fear: it is that exact concentration which allows one to advance where carelessness would be fatal.

Image. Above, Heaven; below, the Lake: the image of treading. Each thing keeps its proper place — heaven remains above, the lake below — and it is this respected order that makes walking possible without danger. This is the figure of the right distance, one that allows advance even near what could bite. Thus the noble one distinguishes high from low, and steadies the resolve of the people: each step placed with discernment, even upon the tiger's tail, becomes a safe step.
